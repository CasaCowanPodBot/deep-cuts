Okay. Let's set the table, because a lot of people are finding this show right now. Mayor of Kingstown premiered on Paramount Plus back in November of twenty twenty-one. It landed on Netflix this October, which is why it's suddenly showing up in everybody's feed. And it's heading toward the finish line. The fifth and final season premieres January fifteenth, with David Morse joining as an F B I agent. So if you want to get caught up, the clock is ticking.

The show comes from Taylor Sheridan, the Yellowstone guy, and Hugh Dillon, who also plays Ian, one of the cops. And the premise is honestly one of the best pitches in the whole Sheridan universe. Kingstown, Michigan is a town where the only industry is incarceration. Seven prisons. Everybody either works in one, has someone inside one, or is about to be in one. And the McLusky family are the power brokers. Officially, they're nothing. Unofficially, they keep the peace between the gangs, the guards, the cops, and the politicians. They are the mayors of Kingstown.

[PAUSE]

And then the pilot does the thing. The thing everyone talks about.

You open the show, you see Kyle Chandler. Coach Taylor. Clear eyes, full hearts. And he's the mayor. Mitch McLusky. He's the guy with all the relationships, all the phone numbers, the guy who seems to be holding this whole rotten place together. And you think, great, this is the Kyle Chandler show.

And then, in the first episode, he's shot in the back of the head in his own office. By a small-time criminal named Alberto, over money Mitch wouldn't let him take.

I want to sit with that for a second, because it's the boldest swing in the season. Paramount is out here marketing a show with Kyle Chandler's face on it, and they kill him in the pilot. It's the Ned Stark move. It's the Executive Decision move, where Steven Seagal dies twenty minutes into the movie. And what it does, structurally, is tell you exactly what kind of show this is. Nobody is safe. Being the good guy, being the smart guy, being the guy everyone respects? None of it protects you in Kingstown.

[PAUSE]

So the job falls to the younger brother. Mike McLusky. Jeremy Renner.

And this is the real story of season one. It's a succession drama. Not the fun Succession, with the insults and the yacht. A grim, gray, snow-covered succession. Mike didn't want the job. He's an ex-con. He's more volatile than Mitch was. And he spends the whole season learning that the job isn't really about power. It's about making promises to people who will absolutely kill you if you break them.

The clearest example is Kenny. Kenny turns himself in, and Mike tries to protect him on the inside. And it backfires horribly. Inmates kill Kenny while the guards just stand there and watch. That's the moment the show tells you what Mike's word is worth when the system doesn't want to honor it. And spoiler, that broken promise is the first domino for everything that happens in the finale.

Renner is really good here, by the way. He plays Mike like a guy who is constantly calculating, and constantly a little bit sick about it. He's not doing Hawkeye. There's no quip. It's a performance built on a tired face and a very quiet voice.

[PAUSE]

Let's do the supporting cast, because this is where the show earns a lot of its points.

Dianne Wiest plays Mariam, the McLusky mom. A teacher who works inside the prison, and who basically disapproves of everything her sons do. Every time she's on screen, the show gets more honest.

Tobi Bamtefa plays Bunny Washington, the leader of the Kingstown Crips. Bunny is one of the best characters on the show, because he and Mike have this alliance that is genuinely respectful and genuinely fragile at the same time.

Emma Laird plays Iris, and she's the most tragic character in the season. She starts as an escort sent to manipulate Mike. She gets trafficked and brutalized by a gang leader named Duke and his crew. Mike eventually kills them and takes her to his cabin.

And then there's Aidan Gillen as Milo Sunter, an imprisoned Russian mob boss. Yes, Littlefinger. Yes, he is once again scheming. And yes, he is once again great at it. Milo spends the season leaning on Mike to recover his hidden fortune, and using Iris as leverage to do it.

[PAUSE]

Now, the dark stuff. Because this show is dark. Like, Taylor Sheridan dark, which is its own unit of measurement.

Episode eight, The Devil Is Us, has the scene everybody remembers. A school bus, full of bodies. It is one of the most upsetting images in any Sheridan show, and that's a crowded field. Whether that kind of brutality is earned, or whether it's just the show trying to out-grim itself, is going to depend on you. I lean earned, mostly, because the show keeps insisting that this violence is the product of a system, not just a few bad guys.

[PAUSE]

Which brings us to the finale. This Piece of My Soul.

Everything boils over. Inmates are furious about the abuse from the guards, and about all those favors that were promised and never delivered after Kenny's death. So they riot. They take guards hostage. An inmate named P-Dog, who is Bunny's cousin, leads it, and he kills a corrections sergeant named Ed Simmons.

And Mike, the guy whose whole job is talking, walks into the middle of it to negotiate.

It doesn't work. Not really. The police, the National Guard, and SWAT storm the prison. P-Dog is killed. A lot of inmates are killed. Mike's brother Kyle and Ian survive a shootout inside. And in the chaos, Milo Sunter walks right out of the prison, disguised as a guard.

So where does that leave us? Mike is now fully the mayor. Iris is under his protection, sitting with him on the floor of his cabin in that final scene. Bunny has lost his man on the inside, and he's starting to wonder whether he can trust Mike. Milo is in the wind. And Kingstown's whole power structure is in pieces.

[PAUSE]

So, the big question. The prestige question. Is Mayor of Kingstown season one actually good?

Here's my case for it. The premise is fantastic. The cast is stacked. The pilot twist is legitimately great television. And underneath all the grit, it's asking a real question about what happens to a place when punishment becomes its whole economy.

Here's the case against. It is relentlessly bleak. It can be heavy-handed. And at times it feels less like it's critiquing violence and more like it's marinating in it.

My verdict? It's not The Wire. It's not trying to be. But it's a much smarter show than its reputation suggests, and season one is a really solid foundation. If you liked the moral grime of Sicario, or the parts of Yellowstone that weren't about horses, this is for you.

And the show seems to have grown into itself. Season four reportedly finished with a perfect score on Rotten Tomatoes. So if season one feels like a strong draft, the evidence says it gets better.

[PAUSE]

One last thought. The title. Mayor of Kingstown. Mitch held the job for years and it killed him in one scene. Mike takes it, and by the end of the season, he's won. He's the mayor. And that might be the worst thing that's ever happened to him.
