title: Lockout Season
description: Baseball's labor deal expires December 1. The owners want a salary cap, the players have never accepted one, and a lockout looks all but certain. What it means for teams, stars, and your offseason.
format: narrative
---
This is Deep Cuts. I'm your narrator. Today: Lockout Season. Baseball's labor war, and what it means for your team and your favorite stars.

[PAUSE]

Let's start with a fun fact that is going to get very awkward in about three minutes. The twenty twenty-six season ended with the Milwaukee Brewers winning the first World Series in franchise history. Milwaukee. One of the smallest markets in the sport. A team that has never, ever been confused with the Dodgers when it comes to spending money.

Hold onto that. Because the entire fight baseball is about to have is built on the idea that teams like the Brewers can't win.

[PAUSE]

Here's the setup. Baseball runs on a collective bargaining agreement, the contract between the owners and the players union. It's more than four hundred pages long, and it covers everything: minimum salaries, free agency, the luxury tax, the draft. The current one covers the twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-six seasons. And it expires at eleven fifty-nine p.m. Eastern time on December first. Two weeks from now.

Nobody thinks they're getting a deal by then. The reporting right now says a new agreement before the deadline is essentially impossible, and that the owners are expected to lock the players out immediately after. In October, both Commissioner Rob Manfred and the union's leader said a temporary extension of the current deal was unlikely too.

A lockout means the sport freezes. No free agent signings. No trades. Nothing. We saw this last time. The last agreement came after a ninety-nine day lockout that pushed back the start of the twenty twenty-two season. And the feeling around baseball back then was: if you think this is bad, wait until twenty twenty-six.

Well. It's twenty twenty-six.

[PAUSE]

So why is this one supposed to be worse? Two words. Salary cap.

In May, the owners proposed a hard salary cap for the first time since the nineteen ninety-four strike. Remember what happened then? The players walked out, the strike lasted seven and a half months, and it wiped out the World Series. The cap was the fight then, and the union won it. Baseball has stayed the only major American league without a cap ever since.

The owners' opening offer looked like this. A cap of two hundred forty-five point three million dollars per team. A floor of one hundred seventy-one point two million, so no team could spend less than that. And a fifty-fifty split of revenue between owners and players. They also want to put all television money under the league.

Then, on November third, the league tweaked it. Same idea, but the cap would start in twenty twenty-eight instead of twenty twenty-seven. The new numbers are a cap of about two hundred fifty-eight million and a floor of about one hundred eighty million. The pitch was that teams need a full offseason to prepare for life under a cap.

The union's response, basically: a delay doesn't change anything. A cap is still a cap.

[PAUSE]

Now, the cap is the headline. But the details are where it really gets wild, especially if you care about stars.

The league has proposed limiting free agent contracts to five years for a player switching teams, topping out at about two hundred two million dollars. If you stay with your own team, you can go six years and around two hundred sixty-five million. And deferred money? Banned entirely.

Think about what that means. Juan Soto's fifteen-year, seven hundred sixty-five million dollar deal with the Mets? That couldn't exist under this system. Shohei Ohtani's contract with the Dodgers, where the vast majority of seven hundred million dollars is deferred for years? Gone. That was the defining contract of this era, and it would be illegal.

Even Alex Rodriguez's old ten-year, two hundred fifty-two million dollar deal from way back in two thousand would break these rules. A contract from the year of Y2K would be too big for the new system.

[PAUSE]

To be fair to the owners, they are offering things in return. They'd raise the minimum salary to one million dollars for players with two years in the big leagues. They'd let players hit free agency a year early, after five seasons instead of six, if they turn thirty that offseason. They'd even end the rule where teams lose draft picks for signing certain free agents.

But here's the catch. Every one of those sweeteners only counts if the union accepts the cap. It's a package deal.

The league's argument is about fairness. Their line is that too many fans in too many markets have too little hope that their team has a fair shot. They say the luxury tax hasn't stopped big spenders, and that football, basketball, and hockey all fixed this with a cap.

And they're pointed about one team in particular. When the union made its own proposal, the league fired back that under the union's plan, the Dodgers would pay less luxury tax and have an extra seventy million dollars to spend on payroll. That's the subtext of this whole fight. The Dodgers are the villain in the owners' story.

[PAUSE]

The players see it completely differently.

First, there's a new person leading them. Tony Clark, the longtime head of the union, resigned in February amid scandal. His deputy, Bruce Meyer, took over on an interim basis. And Meyer is not new to this. He was the union's lead negotiator during the last lockout.

Meyer's argument is simple. Baseball doesn't have a competitive balance problem. It has a spending problem. He points to rising attendance, rising TV ratings, and rising franchise values. And the union's go-to line is that every team in baseball can afford to compete, and many are just choosing not to.

They also put a price tag on it. According to the union, if the owners' fifty-fifty split had been in place this year, players would have lost more than half a billion dollars.

So the players came in with their own offer. No cap. Instead, something they call a competitive-integrity tax, which basically works like a soft floor, penalizing teams that won't spend. They want the luxury tax threshold pushed up closer to three hundred million. And more money for young players.

And that brings us back to Milwaukee. The owners' whole case is that small markets can't win. Then a small market won the World Series. That is going to come up at the bargaining table. A lot.

[PAUSE]

So what does this actually mean for your team?

If you're a big spender, like the Dodgers, the Mets, the Yankees, or the Phillies, the next twelve months are a weird window. Under the league's latest plan, you could keep spending under the old luxury tax system in twenty twenty-seven, but you'd need to start planning for a hard ceiling the year after.

If you're a team that hasn't spent much, the floor is the scary part. Under a floor, you'd have to spend around one hundred eighty million dollars whether you're trying to win or not.

And some teams saw this coming. Cubs president Jed Hoyer admitted that a lot of his contracts were built to expire after twenty twenty-six, right when the agreement does, so the team would have clean books for whatever comes next.

Right now, there's also a mini sprint happening. Free agency is open, and teams are trying to get deals done before December first, because once the lockout hits, the market freezes. The name to watch is Tarik Skubal, the best left-handed pitcher in baseball. The Mets, Padres, Blue Jays, and Dodgers have all reportedly shown interest. And there are reports the Dodgers would prefer a shorter deal. Which makes sense. Nobody knows what a contract is going to be worth in a capped world.

[PAUSE]

And here's the wild card. The Wall Street Journal reported that the league is preparing for a scenario where the players' union, deep into a lockout, files what's called a disclaimer of interest. That means the union would temporarily stop being a union, which would open the door for players to sue the owners under antitrust law. One sports law professor called it the nuclear option. It's theoretical for now. But the fact that the league is planning for it tells you how far apart these two sides are.

[PAUSE]

So where does this leave us? Baseball has had nine work stoppages since nineteen seventy-two. It has not lost regular season games since that nineteen ninety-four strike, which, again, was the last time the owners pushed for a cap.

History doesn't repeat, but it does rhyme. The owners want a cap. The players have never given one up. And the deadline is two weeks away.

My advice? Enjoy the hot stove while it lasts. On December second, it probably goes cold. And nobody knows for how long.

[PAUSE]

That's it for this episode of Deep Cuts. Thanks for listening.
